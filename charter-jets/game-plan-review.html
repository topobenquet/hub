Review of the 2026 PPC Game Plan

You sent through a twelve month PPC game plan, and asked us to be critical of it. This is that response: what we are adopting, what we are changing, and the reasoning behind each change. Where we disagree, the arithmetic is shown so you can check it rather than take our word for it.

What the plan gets right

This is worth saying first, because it is true and because it shapes everything below.

The plan identifies the search term report as the core discipline. Your line that the search term report after launch will teach us more about negative keywords than any research tool beforehand is exactly correct, and it is the habit that separates a healthy account from one that quietly bleeds. Most plans we see treat negatives as a setup task.

It measures the right thing at the end. Naming cost per booked flight, rather than cost per lead, as the number that actually matters is the correct frame for this business.

The industry negative list is well chosen. fractional ownership, jet card, aircraft for sale and flight school are the four categories that drain budget in this vertical, and you identified them without seeing the data. When we classified the account's 1,870 real search terms, those categories showed up almost exactly where you predicted.

The disagreements below are about sequencing and arithmetic, not about judgement.

1. The plan assumes tracking exists. It does not.

Month 1 of the plan says "verify all conversion tracking is firing correctly." There is nothing to verify. As measured on August 31:

Component Actual state
GA4 No property exists
Google Tag Manager No container exists
Google Ads conversions The ones that exist count clicks inside a search widget, see the audit
Call tracking Does not exist
Landing page lead capture Only just built. Before that, form submissions were discarded

Your Step 5 of 7, Tracking Verification, is really step 0, and it is not verification. It is construction from nothing. Steps 1 through 4 (competitor research, keywords, ad copy, landing page) can all be done in parallel, but until measurement exists none of them can be evaluated.

What changes: the order inverts. Measurement is built first, and nothing goes live until it is confirmed working. The full list is in what we need.

2. The two benchmarks in the plan cannot both be true

The plan sets two targets:

  • Cost per call: under $150
  • Cost per booked flight: $500 or less

Together those imply that one in every 3.3 calls becomes a booked flight, a 30% close rate on cold traffic from Google. In brokered charter, where a buyer almost always collects two or three competing quotes before deciding, a realistic first-trip close rate sits between 5% and 15%.

Holding cost per call at $150:

Close rate Cost per booked flight
30% (the plan's implied assumption) $500
15% $1,000
10% $1,500
5% $3,000

Updated with your numbers

You have since given us the average trip value, $20,000, and the average margin, 10%. That is $2,000 of gross margin per booked flight, and it settles this question.

Your $500 target is well chosen. Against $2,000 of margin it is 25% of gross margin, which is a healthy acquisition ratio. The target was never the problem.

The problem is the distance between $500 and what paid search will realistically deliver. At a $14 cost per click, reaching $500 per booked flight requires a click to booking rate of 2.8%. The realistic range is 0.2% to 0.9%. The target is right, and the path to it is three to ten times harder than the plan assumes.

More consequentially, $10,000 a month of media has to produce 5 booked flights to break even, which requires a 17.5% lead to booking close rate. At a normal 10% close rate the program produces about 2.9 flights and loses roughly $4,300 a month on media alone.

Full model, sensitivity tables and the four levers that change it: unit economics. It is now the most important document in this hub, and it changes several things in the plan below.

3. Month 1 at $5,000, split across two search engines, will not produce data

The plan proposes month 1 at $5,000: roughly $3,500 on Google Search, $1,000 on Microsoft Ads, $500 held back.

At the real cost per click for this vertical on exact match keywords, between $8 and $25:

$3,500 on Google
Clicks in the month 140 to 440
At a 3% to 5% inquiry rate 4 to 22 leads in the entire month

Four to twenty-two leads is not enough to decide anything. Not enough to compare two ad variants, not enough to judge an ad group, and not enough for automated bidding to learn. Google's own guidance is that target CPA bidding needs roughly 30 conversions in 30 days.

The $1,000 on Microsoft makes this worse rather than diversifying it. Microsoft is roughly 3% to 6% of US search volume. Putting a fifth of the budget there in month 1 buys statistical noise on two platforms instead of signal on one.

What changes:

  • Month 1 runs 100% on Google Search. Microsoft is mirrored once the Google structure is validated, which we expect around month 3, using the structure we already know works.
  • Budget starts at the $10,000 already committed, not $5,000. At $5,000 spread across four states there is no signal in any of them.
  • Bidding starts on manual cost per click, moving to automated bidding only once there are 30 or more real conversions in a month. The plan does not specify a bidding strategy, and it is one of the largest financial decisions in the build.

4. Performance Max in month 3 is the most expensive available mistake

The plan proposes evaluating Performance Max as a secondary campaign layer in month 3. We recommend against it, firmly, and the reasoning is specific:

  1. Performance Max allocates spend on its own toward the cheapest available inventory, which is Display and Discovery, not Search. In a lead generation account with no lead quality signal, that produces a flood of low quality form fills at an apparently excellent cost per conversion.
  2. Performance Max optimises toward whatever conversion you declare. If the declared conversion is "submitted the form", it will find the cheapest people in the world who submit forms. None of them charter aircraft.
  3. It does not expose search terms at the level a Search campaign does, so it breaks the exact discipline your plan correctly identifies as the most important ongoing task.
  4. This has already happened in this account. There is a paused Performance Max campaign in the history that spent $782.43 running against the false conversions described in the audit.

What changes: Performance Max stays off until two conditions are met together: 50 or more real conversions per month, and offline conversion import from your CRM, so that Google can tell the difference between a lead that bought and a lead that did not. Before that, it is not a secondary layer. It is a leak.

5. The plan is built around the phone call, but never builds call tracking

From "Months 6 to 9" onward, most of the plan turns on the phone: scaling call-only campaigns, call extensions on every campaign, call recording, tuning ad scheduling to the best call conversion windows, tightening geography by caller quality.

We agree with the premise. In charter, the call is very likely the highest value lead.

The problem is that call tracking appears once in the whole document, as half a line inside Step 5, and the budget allocation never funds it. You cannot "refine ad scheduling around your highest call conversion windows" without knowing which call came from which keyword.

There is also a detail the plan does not account for: Google's forwarding numbers only cover calls originating from the ad, and from the website if the call conversion snippet is installed. Calls arriving through charterjetsinc.com, which is a separate property, fall outside that, and the same number is published on both.

What changes: call tracking becomes a launch blocker rather than a month 6 task. It is the only way the plan's own target of under $150 per call can be measured at all.

6. The geographic priority conflicts with the only asset that exists

The plan prioritises South Florida, New York and Los Angeles from month 1, opening Dallas in month 5.

The campaign landing page that exists today is titled Private Jet Rentals in Miami. The headline says Miami, the routes are outbound from Miami, and the airports section covers the four Miami and Fort Lauderdale fields.

Sending someone who searched private jet charter NYC to a Miami page damages two of the three components of Quality Score at once: ad relevance and landing page experience. That raises cost per click and lowers ad position, so it is a direct financial cost, not a cosmetic one.

Two ways forward, and we need you to pick one:

Resolved: we launch in two markets, Florida and New York.

The market research settled it. City qualified keywords barely exist in this vertical, so geography is set by campaign targeting rather than by keyword, which means at $10,000 a month we are budget constrained rather than demand constrained. Narrowing geography costs nothing in reachable volume.

Markets Clicks per market per day
4 states about 6, too thin to learn anywhere
2 states about 12
1 state about 24

Two rather than one, because Florida is where the dominant direct competitor is based, so Florida alone would only tell us how we perform in our opponent's strongest market. Florida and New York are also two ends of the same corridor rather than unrelated markets.

The condition is that New York gets its own landing page before launch. If it is not ready, we open Florida only and add New York when it is. Texas and California follow in month 5 on the proven template.

What the plan does not cover

# Gap Why it matters
1 Lead response time commitment The landing page currently promises a specialist responds in 15 minutes. Nobody has agreed to that. In charter, response speed moves the close rate more than anything we can do inside the ad account
2 Responsive search ad requirements Step 3 asks for 3 headline variations per ad group. A responsive search ad accepts up to 15 headlines and 4 descriptions, and at 3 headlines Google rates the ad strength as Poor. The full set is written and ready in ad copy
3 The domain Ads would point at a subdomain we own rather than one you own. Lower trust for the person clicking, and the asset does not end up belonging to Charter Jets
4 Whether we bid on competitor brand names Bidding on Monarch Air Group or Mercury Jets is inexpensive, permitted in the US provided the trademark does not appear in the ad text, and it converts. The plan studies competitors thoroughly but never decides whether to buy their traffic
5 Ad scheduling as a lever, not a refinement The plan treats it as routine bid adjustment. At roughly a twelfth of Monarch's budget we cannot hold top of page all day. Concentrating spend into the hours your team actually answers the phone is one of the highest impact decisions available, and it belongs on day 1
6 Avinode commission and currency The search widget currently carries a 0% commission, meaning it displays wholesale operator pricing. Any paid traffic landing there before that is corrected is wasted at the worst possible moment in the funnel

On the competitor research tools

The plan recommends SpyFu, SEMrush, SimilarWeb and iSpionage. Together that is more than $500 a month of subscriptions against a $5,000 media budget, and they overlap heavily.

What is actually needed is covered like this:

Need Tool Cost
See the live ads Monarch, Mercury, Victor, Air Charter Service and BitLux are running Google Ads Transparency Center Free, and it is first party data
Search volume and estimated cost per click Keyword Planner, inside the account Free
Competitor keyword and ad copy history One month of SEMrush, extract everything, cancel One month
What landing pages and calls to action they use Manual capture of the results page Free

One month of one paid tool, not four ongoing subscriptions. That money is better spent on media.

On the competitor spend benchmarks

The plan benchmarks against VistaJet at $250,000 to $450,000 a month and FlexJet at $300,000 to $500,000. Those are not comparable businesses. They are operators with jet card and fractional ownership programs and national brand budgets. They are not selling the same product.

The comparable businesses are Monarch at $60,000 to $80,000 and Mercury at $50,000 to $80,000, and even those spend five to eight times what we will.

The useful conclusion from that table, which the plan does not draw, is strategic: we cannot compete broadly. There is no version of this where we hold meaningful impression share on a generic term like private jet charter. We have to win narrowly: a small set of routes and high intent terms, in the hours your team is answering, on exact match. That is what the campaign structure is built to do.

One thing worth agreeing on in advance

The first month's report will look worse than what you are seeing today. We will go from thousands of recorded conversions to a few dozen real leads, and from thousands of cheap clicks to hundreds of expensive ones.

That is the intended outcome, not a setback. But if it arrives without having been said first, it reads like a disaster instead of what it is: the first time this account has measured something true.
