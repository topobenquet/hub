Unit Economics

You gave us the two numbers that were blocking everything: average trip value of $20,000 and average margin of 10%. That is $2,000 of gross margin per booked flight.

This document works out what that means for the program. It is now the most important page in this hub, because every other decision, budget, geography, bidding, which keywords we are willing to pay for, follows from it.

Rewritten September 3, 2026. The original version of this page modelled the funnel on industry estimates because we had nothing else. We now have $67,621 of real spend from account 645-044-1013 to calibrate against, and the real numbers are materially worse than the estimates. Everything below now uses measured data. The background is in account history.

The headline: at the capture rate this business actually achieved, paid search does not pay for itself. Fixing that is not about bidding. It is about roughly doubling how many of the clicks we already pay for turn into a lead, and that is squarely what the landing page and call tracking work is for.

Where it breaks even

At the 15% markup now live in the Avinode widget, each booked flight carries $3,000 of gross margin, so the media needs 3.33 booked flights a month to pay for itself. Combining capture rate and close rate:

Capture rate Inquiries At 15% close At 20% close At 30% close
0.66% (measured) 9.1 1.4 flights, -$5,898 1.8 flights, -$4,530 2.7 flights, -$1,796
1.00% 13.8 2.1 flights, -$3,785 2.8 flights, -$1,713 4.1 flights, +$2,431
1.20% 16.6 2.5 flights, -$2,541 3.3 flights, break even 5.0 flights, +$4,917
1.50% 20.7 3.1 flights, -$677 4.1 flights, +$2,431 6.2 flights, +$8,646

Read the top row. At the capture rate this account actually achieved, breaking even would require closing 37% of inquiries, which nobody does on cold inbound.

Now read the third row. Lift capture from 0.66% to 1.2%, roughly double, and a normal 20% close rate breaks even. Get to 1.5% and a 16% close rate breaks even.

So the whole programme reduces to one question: can we double the share of paid clicks that become a measurable lead? That is not a bidding problem, and no amount of keyword work solves it. It is the landing page and the measurement.

What this changes about your game plan

Your plan set two targets: under $150 per call and $500 or less per booked flight.

With these economics, the $500 target is actually well chosen. Against $2,000 of margin, $500 is 25% of gross margin, which is a healthy acquisition ratio. The problem was never that number.

The problem is the distance between $500 and what paid search will realistically deliver. Getting to $500 per booked flight from a $14 click requires a click to booking rate of 2.8%. The realistic range is 0.2% to 0.9%. The target is right and the path to it is three to ten times harder than the plan assumes.

The four levers, ranked by how much they move

1. Margin. The largest single lever, and it is not a marketing decision.

Close rate required to break even, at each markup and each capture rate:

Markup Margin per flight Flights needed At 0.66% capture At 1.2% capture
10% $2,000 5.0 55% 30%
15%, now live $3,000 3.33 37% 20%
20% $4,000 2.5 27% 15%

You have already pulled this lever: the Avinode commission went from 0% to 15% on September 2. That moved the required close rate at a 1.2% capture rate from 30% down to 20%, which is the difference between impossible and ordinary.

A 10% markup on a $20,000 charter is at the low end for a broker. If that number is set by competitive pressure rather than by policy, it is worth revisiting, because at 10% the paid acquisition channel has very little room to work with.

One question we need answered: is the 10% gross margin on the full $20,000, meaning $2,000 before your own costs, or is it net of everything? The whole model above assumes the former. If it is net, the picture is better than shown. If $2,000 is before staff, software and overhead, it is worse.

Update: this is now live. The Avinode commission was set to 15% on September 2 and verified. That is the same decision as raising the markup, because that setting determines the price the client is shown. It is the largest single improvement available to this program:

At 15% the program turns profitable at a 12% close rate instead of an 18% one, which moves it from demanding to reasonable.

One thing to hold to now that it is live: the 15% has to be the price your specialists actually honour on the quote. The widget now shows wholesale plus 15%. If a specialist then quotes something different, we have recreated the price mismatch at the worst point in the funnel, with the lead already warm. Worth a note to the charter desk.

2. Repeat business. This is what most likely makes the program work.

At a 1.2% capture rate and a 20% close rate, acquiring a client costs about $3,000, and each flight carries $3,000 of margin at the 15% markup.

Flights per client, lifetime Lifetime margin Acquisition cost as % of it Verdict
1 $3,000 100% Break even, no more
2 $6,000 50% Works
3 $9,000 33% Works well
4 $12,000 25% Works very well

If a typical client flies twice, the program works. If most clients fly once and disappear, it does not. That single number decides whether this channel is viable, and it is the one number we still do not have. It is now the most valuable thing you can send us.

3. The close rate itself, which is mostly about speed

The gap between a 10% and a 20% close rate is the difference between losing $4,300 a month and making $1,400. Nothing in the ad account moves that. What moves it:

  • How fast a lead gets a human response. The landing page currently promises 15 minutes. If that is real, it is worth more than any bid adjustment we could make.
  • Whether calls are answered. A call is the highest intent signal available, and calls typically close several times better than form fills.
  • Whether the quote is competitive, which loops back to the markup question.

This is why the response time commitment appears in what we need. It is not administrative. It is a P&L input.

4. The capture rate. On the measured numbers, this is now the whole game.

Going from the measured 0.66% to 1.2% cuts cost per inquiry from $1,097 to $603 and moves the programme from structurally unprofitable to break even at an ordinary close rate. No other lever available to us is worth as much.

That reframes the work in landing and conversion from cosmetic to load bearing. Concretely, the old landing page captured only the Avinode widget inquiry. The new one captures three paths, its own short form with the click identifier attached, the Avinode inquiry, and, once call tracking is in, the phone. Counting all three should get us a meaningful part of the way to 1.2% before we improve anything else, because some of those leads were always happening and simply were not being counted.

What we are changing as a result

  1. Every keyword now has a cost ceiling attached to it. At $350 per lead we cannot afford to buy curiosity. The price research campaign, which we had capped at $15 a day as a small experiment, is now on a much shorter leash. It gets 30 days to produce a booked flight or it goes off.
  2. Call tracking moves from important to non-negotiable. If calls close at 20% or 25% while forms close at 5%, then optimising toward form fills actively destroys value. We cannot know without measuring, and today we cannot measure.
  3. Offline conversion import stops being a phase 2 nicety. With a 17.5% required close rate, Google has to be optimising toward booked flights, not leads. That requires the sales outcome to flow back into the account.
  4. Concentrating geography gets easier to justify. Details in the market research, but at this cost per lead we cannot afford thin coverage in four markets.
  5. Repeat and referral move up the roadmap. In your plan they sit in months 9 to 12. If repeat business is what makes the unit economics work, it deserves attention from month 1, and it is far cheaper than buying a new client at $3,500.

Send those two and we can replace every estimate on this page with your actual numbers, and set a target cost per lead we can both hold the campaign to.

An honest summary

At a $20,000 ticket, paid search for charter only works if two things hold: the markup stays at 15%, which you have now done, and the landing page captures roughly twice the share of clicks the old one did.

Neither is a bidding problem. The account we are inheriting already had sound tracking, correct geographic targeting and disciplined negatives, and it still lost money, because it sent good traffic to a page that captured 0.66% of it and then, for the last five months, to a page that did not load at all.

So the programme should be built and judged on capture and response, not on cost per click: send less traffic to a better page, count every path it produces, answer fast, and treat the second flight from the same client as part of the acquisition maths rather than a bonus.
